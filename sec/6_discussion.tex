\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the results show.}
A VBTS image contains the hardware state of its unit and gel. In our recordings the state was stable over months of use and was replaced with the unit or the gel. When classes are tied to hardware, as one object per session ties them, a classifier uses the state as a class feature. This holds on our recordings and in SITR when we inject the tie. A robot can undo much of the damage with its own unlabelled contacts. Reading each image relative to a running estimate of the state removes the smooth, additive part of the state without any training. It does as well as the best test-time adaptation method we tested, without batches or gradient steps, and it improves the published domain adaptation methods we added it to. Aligning the encoder with the contacts recorded after the change removes more, and it also helps where two sensors differ in ways that no correction of the image can undo.

\paragraph{What they do not show.}
The method repairs the shortcut, not every effect of new hardware. The reading lowers the accuracy on the eight sessions recorded on the other gel of the same unit, both alone and on top of alignment. These are four rigid materials, tested in the campaigns that follow and precede our gel replacement, and their sessions are among the first on their hardware, where the estimate holds few other objects. A better estimate does not help. Keeping the unit's estimate across the gel replacement, on the reasoning that the smooth field belongs mostly to the unit, lowers this stratum further. On these sessions the state is a valid cue for the classes learned under it, and any reading removes part of it. Alignment needs a stream that covers the materials the robot is expected to recognise. On SITR, a stream of 5 of the 20 classes gives less than half of its gain. In our main protocol the stream contains the test sessions themselves, and alignment with only the first half of each hardware state's sessions keeps most of the gain on the second half. On whole-frame crops the reading does not help, because those crops contain the illumination field that the classifier may already have learned as a class feature. HCT, the hand-held DIGIT data of TVL~\cite{tvl2024}, ties materials to sessions as well, but the shift between its recording days is mostly a change of objects and of the mix of materials rather than of the sensor's look, and our method does not help there (supplement).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
We followed two units and three gels of one sensor model. The public datasets add seven further GelSight Minis and hand-held DIGIT data, and only Tactile MNIST and HCT span more than a few days. No material was recorded on the same unit and gel months apart, so stability over months is shown for the state itself and not for a classifier. Lubricant is re-applied in step with use, so slow drift with use cannot be separated from lubricant residue. The estimate counts every session once and so needs to know when the robot moves to a new object, which a robot knows from its task. Absolute accuracies are modest for a 20-way problem on single crops with one session per material.

\paragraph{Outlook.}
Every GelSight Mini dataset we examined carries its hardware history in its images, and evaluations within one collection cannot see it. Holding out sessions recorded on other hardware, reporting the share of test samples in the most predicted class, and reading every image together with the state of the sensor that took it are simple practices. They make this failure visible and, to a large extent, repairable with the robot's own contacts.

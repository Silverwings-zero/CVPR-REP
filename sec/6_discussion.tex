\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What this means for evaluation.}
A tactile classifier can be accurate for the wrong reason. When each class is recorded on one piece of hardware, the mark of that hardware identifies the class, and a split of the same recordings into training and test sets carries the mark on both sides. Such a split rewards a model for using the mark and cannot reveal that it does. The dependence shows only when the hardware changes. To expose it earlier, a dataset should record which unit and gel produced every image, and an evaluation should hold out hardware and report how the predictions spread over the classes. Pretrained encoders are not exempt, since a classifier on frozen Sparsh features failed in the same way. A system that learns from touch should treat its sensor's state as part of its input.

\paragraph{When the reading helps and when alignment is needed.}
The reading corrects the input. It removes one component, a smooth image added to every contact on the same hardware, so it helps where that component separates two sensors, as it does our two units, and it costs no training. SITR's Minis show where it stops. They also differ in field of view and contrast, which change each image in its own way, so no single image subtracted from all of them can remove the difference. A frozen encoder, which never learned these differences, recovers once the mean image is subtracted. A network trained from scratch learns them, and it does not. Alignment acts on the features rather than the pixels, so it reaches these differences and the fine pattern, which is why it adds to the reading even between our two units. Its price is retraining.

\paragraph{The gel-only sessions.}
On the eight sessions that cross only a gel, the reading lowers the accuracy, alone and on top of alignment. We observe this drop, but eight sessions do not establish its cause. They hold four rigid materials, each among the first sessions on its gel, so the material, the gel and the age of the estimate change together. One possibility is that the shortcut is right here: these materials were learned on the unit that tests them, so the illumination that misleads the classifier elsewhere points to the correct classes. Another is that a new gel changes the image in ways the smooth component does not carry. We therefore claim no benefit from the reading after a change of gel alone.

\paragraph{Practical limits.}
The estimate separates the hardware from the touch only by averaging over different objects, so a robot that handles one object, or a few alike, would subtract part of their imprint, and the robot must know when its object changes. Alignment asks for more: on SITR its gain halves when the stream holds a quarter of the classes. A stable mark is also not a stable classifier. No material was recorded on the same unit and gel months apart, so recognition over that time is untested, and because lubricant is re-applied in step with use, wear cannot be separated from residue. We followed two units and three gels of one sensor model, and on HCT~\cite{tvl2024}, where the shift between days is mostly a change of objects, the method does not help (supplement).

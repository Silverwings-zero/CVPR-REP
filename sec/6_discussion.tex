\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the representation is.}
A tactile representation for robot learning has to carry the sensor's state, because the state belongs to the unit and the gel, survives months of use, and dominates the fine-scale content of every image. Ours factors each observation into state and touch and keeps two states the robot maintains from its own stream---the loaded state, tracked from recent grasps and restarted when the hardware changes, and the unloaded state from the bare gel between grasps---and reads each cue against the right one: appearance against the loaded state, geometry and contact against the unloaded one. This is general in the ways that matter downstream: it applies on top of any encoder, including frozen foundation models, it serves the three quantities a grasping policy needs, it can be learned without labels, and it transferred to a second sensor unit. Its gain is specific: a rendering state in which the test classes were never imaged, which collapses a classifier onto the classes that were---the situation one-object-per-session recording produces whenever a unit or gel is changed mid-dataset. Within known states the subtraction is unnecessary and can cost a few points, on a single-unit training set it adds 9--17 points to a plain CNN and nothing to one trained with photometric jitter, and it did not recover the rigid materials across the gel replacement. It is deliberately not a single network that absorbs all three cues: every attempt to learn force from appearance, including with the force signal in the objective, failed across days, and factoring the cues is the stronger design.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Our recordings come from two units and three gels of one sensor model. In public data the state is identifiable from the bare frame of every unit and gel we looked at, the tracker detects a gel swap on another lab's sensor, and the stream estimate carries recognition across GelSight Minis for a frozen encoder; it does not carry force across a change of gel there either, and the label-free swap does not transfer to coarse shape. No material was recorded on the same unit and gel months apart, so the stability of the state over months is shown for the state itself, not for a classifier. The materials tested across the change of unit are the fifteen textured ones and those tested across the gel replacement the five rigid ones, so the gel's mechanics and the materials' lack of fine texture cannot be separated as the reason the correction stops there. Each session holds one material, so material identity can only be tested across campaigns. Absolute accuracies are modest for a 20-way zero-shot problem; the headline gains are gains against the collapse, and without it the state change still costs 10--20 points that no state estimate recovers; a few target-campaign labels stack with it. Every design choice was selected on campaign D; the other three folds are independent of that but contain no second unseen-state test on the same gel. The label-free objective is weak from scratch; its value is on a pretrained encoder, and we have shown it for texture only. Lubricant is re-applied in step with use, so slow drift with use cannot be separated from lubricant residue.

\paragraph{Outlook.}
The sensor's state is present in every VBTS dataset recorded in sittings, which is all of them, and it is invisible to within-collection evaluation; a dataset's own images record every change of its hardware, as ours did when its log did not, and a robot can read that record as it happens. Representing the state explicitly, holding out sessions recorded on other hardware, and estimating the state from the deployment's own contacts where no blank frames exist are cheap changes that we hope become standard.

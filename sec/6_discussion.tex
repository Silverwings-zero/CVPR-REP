\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What this means for evaluation.}
A tactile classifier can be accurate for the wrong reason. When each class is recorded on one piece of hardware, the signature of that hardware identifies the class, and a train/test split of the same recordings carries the signature on both sides. Such a split rewards a model for using the signature and cannot reveal that it does. To expose the dependence before the hardware changes, a dataset should record which unit and gel produced every image, and an evaluation should hold out hardware and report how the predictions spread over the classes. Pretrained encoders are not exempt: a classifier on frozen Sparsh features failed in the same way. A system that learns from touch should treat its sensor's state as part of its input.

\paragraph{When correction helps and when alignment is needed.}
Image correction removes one component, a smooth image added to every contact on the same hardware. It helps where that component separates two sensors, as with our two units, and costs no training. SITR's Minis also differ in field of view and contrast, which change each image in its own way, so no single subtracted image can remove the difference. A frozen encoder, which never learned these differences, recovers once the mean image is subtracted, and a network trained from scratch does not. Alignment acts on the features rather than the pixels, so it reaches these differences and the fine pattern and adds to the correction even between our two units, at the price of retraining.

\paragraph{The gel-only sessions.}
On the eight sessions that cross only a gel, the correction lowers the accuracy, alone and on top of alignment. We observe this drop, but eight sessions do not establish its cause. They hold four rigid materials, each among the first sessions on its gel, so material, gel and the age of the estimate change together. Perhaps the shortcut is right here: these materials were learned on the unit that tests them, so the illumination that misleads the classifier elsewhere points to the correct classes. Or a new gel changes the image in ways the smooth component does not carry. We therefore claim no benefit after a gel change alone.

\paragraph{Practical limits.}
The estimate separates the hardware from the touch only by averaging over different objects, so a robot that handles one object, or a few alike, would subtract part of their imprint, and it must know when its object changes. Alignment asks for more: on SITR its gain halves when the stream holds a quarter of the classes. A stable signature is also not a stable classifier. No material was recorded on the same unit and gel months apart, so recognition over that time is untested, and wear cannot be separated from lubricant residue. We followed two units and three gels of one sensor model, and on HCT~\cite{tvl2024}, where days differ mainly in objects, the method does not help (supplement).

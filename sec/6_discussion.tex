\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What this means for evaluation.}
High accuracy on a standard split does not show that a tactile classifier recognizes the material. When each class is recorded on one piece of hardware, the signature of that hardware identifies the class, and a train/test split of the same recordings carries the signature on both sides, so it rewards the shortcut and cannot reveal it. To expose the dependence before the hardware changes, a dataset should record which unit and gel produced every image, and an evaluation should hold out hardware and report how predictions spread over classes.

\paragraph{The gel-only sessions.}
On the eight sessions recorded after a gel change on the same unit, the correction lowers the accuracy, alone and on top of alignment. These sessions hold four rigid materials, each among the first on its gel, so material, gel and the age of the estimate change together, and eight sessions do not establish the cause. The shortcut may happen to be right here, because these materials were recorded for training on the unit that tests them. A new gel may also change the image beyond its smooth component. We therefore claim no benefit after a gel change alone.

\paragraph{Practical limits.}
The estimate separates the hardware from the touch only by averaging over different objects, so a robot that handles one object, or a few alike, would subtract part of their imprint, and it must know when its object changes. Alignment needs a stream that covers the robot's materials (\cref{sec:second}). A stable hardware signature does not imply a stable classifier. No material was recorded on the same unit and gel months apart, so recognition over months is untested, and wear cannot be separated from lubricant residue. We followed two units and three gels of one sensor model, and on HCT~\cite{tvl2024}, where days differ mainly in objects, the method does not help (\supp{T}).

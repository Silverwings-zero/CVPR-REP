\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The gel-only sessions.}
On the eight sessions that differ from their training data only in the gel, the correction lowers the accuracy, alone and on top of alignment. These sessions hold four rigid materials, each among the first on its gel, so material, gel and the age of the estimate change together, and eight sessions do not establish the cause. A new gel also changes the image beyond its smooth component. Re-rendering the July sessions with the difference between the two gels' bare-gel frames raises a CNN from \GelRrCSupRaw\ to \GelRrCSupRr\pct\ (\supp{M}). For a change of gel alone, the contact estimate is therefore not enough, and a bare-gel frame helps.

\paragraph{Practical limits.}
The estimate separates the hardware from the touch only by averaging over different objects, so a robot that handles one object, or a few alike, would subtract part of their imprint, and it must know when its object changes. Alignment needs a stream that covers the robot's materials (\cref{sec:second}). No material was recorded on the same unit and gel months apart, so recognition over months is untested, and wear cannot be separated from lubricant residue. The failure we study needs the new hardware to be tied to a few classes in the training set, as it is in every fold. We followed two units and three gels of one sensor model, with one change of unit and one of gel, and on HCT~\cite{tvl2024}, where days differ mainly in objects, the method lowers accuracy (\supp{T}).

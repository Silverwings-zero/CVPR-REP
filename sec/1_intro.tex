\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A vision-based tactile sensor (VBTS) presses a soft elastomer gel onto a surface and photographs the gel's deformation with a camera behind it~\cite{yuan2017gelsight,dong2017improved,lambeta2020digit}. One touch thus records the shape of the contact, the forces on it, and the texture of the surface at a resolution of tens of micrometers. These images are used to predict whether a grasp will hold~\cite{calandra2017feeling}, detect slip~\cite{dong2017improved}, perceive and manipulate objects in the hand~\cite{she2021cable,suresh2024neuralfeels,qi2023rotateit} and recognize what is touched~\cite{li2013texture,yuan2017connecting,yang2022touchandgo}. Because the output is an image, the models come from computer vision, and encoders pretrained on large collections of tactile frames now serve all of these tasks~\cite{higuera2024sparsh,t3fota2024,unitouch2024,anytouch2025}.

This paper studies one of these tasks, recognizing the material of a surface from a single grasp. Knowing the material helps a robot predict slip, choose a grip force and sort objects. Material recognition is a standard benchmark for tactile encoders~\cite{higuera2024sparsh,unitouch2024,anytouch2025}, and the task most exposed to the problem we describe. The shape of a contact changes the image strongly, but materials differ mainly in fine, low-contrast detail, and the sensor adds fine detail of its own. A robot keeps its sensors for months, during which the gel is lubricated and worn, and eventually the gel, a consumable part, is replaced~\cite{davis2025resilience,cottrell2026durable}, as is a unit that fails. A classifier that depends on the hardware fails after such routine maintenance, and without labels the failure goes unnoticed.

The camera, the LEDs and the gel cartridge leave a faint mark of their own in every image, which we call its \emph{hardware state} or signature. It has two parts, a fine pattern carried by the gel cartridge and a smooth, uneven illumination. We recorded \EpRecorded\ grasps of 20 materials over four months with two GelSight Mini units and three gels, and \cref{fig:signature} shows how strong the fine pattern is in them. At a fixed image position, the fine detail of a contact correlates at \HwFarWeeks\ with other objects pressed on the same unit and gel days to weeks later and at \HwFarMonths\ three months later, but only at \HwFarOtherUnit\ and \HwFarOtherGel\ with a different unit or gel. A model trained to recognize materials therefore also sees cues identifying the sensor hardware; we investigate whether it relies on these cues and how that affects recognition when the hardware changes.

Tactile pipelines already compensate for the sensor's appearance (\cref{sec:related}), but no study follows one unit and gel for months or tests whether a classifier uses the hardware as a cue for the class. In medical imaging, a network trained to detect pneumonia in chest X-rays learned hospital-specific cues and lost accuracy at previously unseen hospitals~\cite{zech2018hospital}.

A classifier can exploit hardware cues when the training data associate particular materials with particular sensors, as happens when each object is recorded in its own session. In our recordings, a ResNet-50 trained on June and July assigns \XpIntroUnitTwoShare\pct\ of the September and October episodes to the four materials that the new unit had recorded in training. Adding the campaign in which the new unit first recorded those four materials lowers a CNN's accuracy on that unit from \ThirteenABtoDRaw\ to \ThirteenABCtoDRaw\pct, or from \ThirteenABtoDAugRaw\ to \ThirteenABCtoDAugRaw\pct\ when it is trained with photometric jitter. Controlled experiments on two public datasets reproduce the collapse, and frozen tactile encoders expose the hardware to a linear probe (\cref{sec:shortcut}).

Existing corrections rely on a frame of the unloaded gel, calibration contacts, simulation, or labeled or paired target data (\cref{sec:related}). We show that, for a change of unit, the robot's ordinary contacts are enough. Because the state is shared by every object, the average of the contacts made since the last hardware change estimates it, and we explain why, for a classifier trained on uncorrected images, only its coarse scales can be subtracted safely (\cref{sec:estimate}). The method therefore subtracts the smooth part of this running estimate from every image (\cref{fig:overview}). This image correction needs no labels or training and can be placed in front of any classifier. If the encoder can be retrained, the same contacts serve as the target domain of a conditional adversary~\cite{long2018cdan}, which can also act on the fine pattern.

We test on 55 sessions in which a material is touched with a different unit or gel than in training. On these sessions, the correction alone raises a ResNet-50 from \XpRecipeAcc\ to \XpReadAcc\pct, which is on par, over the pooled sessions, with the best of five published test-time adaptation methods, and it improves each of five published domain adaptation methods. With alignment it reaches \XpFullAcc\pct, and the gains hold for four encoders on crops along the contact. The correction has limits. It lowers the accuracy on the eight sessions that differ from their training data only in the gel, and it removes only an additive component, so a CNN trained from scratch on SITR's Minis, which also differ in field of view and contrast, needs alignment.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item A four-month measurement showing that the hardware state is present in the bare gel, stable with use, and replaced along with the unit or gel.
  \item Evidence that classifiers use this state as a shortcut when materials are tied to hardware, on our recordings and, under control, on two public datasets, and that frozen encoders expose it to a linear probe.
  \item A method that estimates the state from the robot's ordinary contacts, without a bare-gel frame, calibration or labels, with an analysis of the scales at which it is reliable.
  \item A four-month dataset of two units, three gels and 20 materials, with force, bare-gel frames, protocol and code.
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A vision-based tactile sensor (VBTS) presses an elastomer gel onto a surface and photographs the deformation under internal LEDs~\cite{yuan2017gelsight,dong2017improved,lambeta2020digit}. The frame is treated as a picture of the contact. It is fed to encoders that read material, force or slip, to foundation models pretrained on millions of such frames~\cite{higuera2024sparsh,t3fota2024,unitouch2024,anytouch2025}, and to policies that run on robot hands for months: DIGIT fingertips track objects in hand~\cite{suresh2024neuralfeels}, multi-fingered hands rotate unseen objects~\cite{qi2023rotateit}, a GelSight Mini conditions a manipulation policy on force~\cite{helmut2025tactilediffusion}, and a multimodal fingertip is sold as a product~\cite{lambeta2024digit360}. Over those months the sensor changes. It is powered off and on, its gel is lubricated and worn, and eventually the gel, or the whole unit, is replaced.

A sensor's state has two parts: how it renders a contact (illumination, optics, camera, the gel's fixed texture) and how its gel deforms. This paper is about the first part. We show that the rendering state is a property of the unit-and-gel pair: it does not move with use over months, it changes when the hardware changes, it is estimable from the frames the sensor produces anyway, and it can be subtracted from the image. The mechanical part changes with the gel; force and contact must then be read as geometry against the unloaded gel.

That the sensor is in the image is known, and three practices deal with it. The first subtracts a blank frame of the unloaded gel, captured at the start of an episode or periodically: GelSlim re-captures it every hundred grasps~\cite{gelslim2018}, TACTIC averages fifty frames per rollout~\cite{tactic2026}, and Sparsh, SITR, AnyTouch~2 and HTT subtract one as preprocessing~\cite{higuera2024sparsh,sitr2025,anytouch2026two,htt2026}. The second diversifies the training data: NeuralFeels renders with the backgrounds of 25 DIGITs and random LEDs~\cite{suresh2024neuralfeels}, TACTIC records with four cartridges, SITR simulates a hundred optical configurations and calibrates each unit with eighteen presses~\cite{sitr2025}. The third avoids the image: RotateIt reduces it to a contact location~\cite{qi2023rotateit}, and a reactive diffusion policy reads marker displacements because they survive ``damage or gel replacements''~\cite{rdp2025}. None of these measures what the blank removes, none follows one unit and gel for months, and none shows what a change of hardware does to a classifier trained, as tactile classifiers are, with one object per session. Every published evaluation is within a collection; the closest proxies swap units~\cite{sitr2025,bideta2026,feats2025,tacverse2026,rct2026} or gels~\cite{anytouch2026two}, or span two weeks~\cite{tactilemnist2025}. Other imaging fields have asked the question: classifiers learn the hospital from an X-ray~\cite{zech2018hospital}, the scanner from an MRI~\cite{fortin2017combat}, the dataset from a photograph~\cite{torralba2011bias,liuhe2025}, and microscopy estimates its flat-field from the images themselves when no blank exists~\cite{singh2014illumination,peng2017basic}. For touch, nobody has.

We recorded 2{,}711 grasps of 20 materials in four campaigns over four months, with two units and three gels, the normal force measured on the opposing finger and, in the last campaign, bare-gel frames between grasps. A probe with no model shows the hardware in the image (\cref{fig:signature}). At a fixed image position, the fine-scale content of a contact correlates with the mean of other contacts at \HwOwn\ for its own session, \HwSameDay\ for another object touched the same day, \HwWeeks\ for the same unit and gel on another day, \HwMonths\ for the same unit and gel three months and some 1{,}300 grasps later, and \HwOtherUnit\ and \HwOtherGel\ for the other unit or the other gel. The pattern is in the bare gel (\BlankVsField), stays fixed in image coordinates while the membrane moves, and is shared most by thick soft samples that cannot transmit a backing: it is the rendering state of the hardware, not the object. It is stable enough, and its breaks sharp enough, that we dated the change of unit and the gel replacement from the images alone and corrected the laboratory log. Its consequence is a shortcut. Each object is recorded in one session, so a material classifier can satisfy its labels with the hardware state, and does. A CNN trained on the June--July campaigns recognises unseen sessions of the same hardware at \FoldBSupRawAcc--\FoldASupRawAcc\pct. Tested in September--October on the second unit, which appears in its training data for four rigid materials only, it assigns \SupFoneRawSTop\pct\ of all episodes to one of those four, recognises the fifteen materials it learned on the first unit at \DOneSupRaw\pct\ (chance 5\pct), and the five it learned on the second unit, now on a replaced gel, at \DTwoSupRaw\pct; frozen Sparsh encoders score \SdinoRawAcc--\IjepaRawAcc\pct. We propose the following interpretation: a classifier trained with one object per session learns the unit as a class feature, and under that unit's state everything looks like the classes recorded on it.

If that is right, removing the state should undo the collapse, and estimating it should not need a blank frame: the position-wise mean of many contacts of different objects contains the hardware and not the objects, the retrospective flat-field of microscopy~\cite{singh2014illumination,peng2017basic}. It does. Subtracting the previous object's mean field restores the fifteen cross-unit materials from \DOneSupRaw\ to \DOneSupPrev\pct\ (a bare-gel frame: \DOneSupBlank\pct), the CNN overall to \SupFonePrevSAcc\pct\ and frozen Sparsh to \IjepaPrevAcc\pct; per-session AdaBN gives \SupFoneAdaSessAcc\pct. The same re-rendering trains a label-free adapter: contacts rendered under other sessions' states as positives, other surfaces under the same state as decoys, which lifts frozen Sparsh to \IjepaAdAcc\pct\ with no target data, against \IjepaAdJitterAcc\pct\ with photometric positives. Holding out each campaign in turn bounds the claim. The correction matters only for a state that is unseen for the test classes, is unnecessary within known states, and did not recover the five rigid materials across the gel replacement (\DTwoSupRaw$\rightarrow$\DTwoSupPrev\pct), where normal force has to be read from marker geometry against the unloaded gel ($r$=\ForceGeomXeraC) and appearance fails ($R^2\le\ForceLearnedBestR$). The pattern and the collapse replicate on another lab's GelSight Mini~\cite{tactilemnist2025}.

Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item We show that a VBTS frame carries the rendering state of its unit and gel: present in the bare gel, fixed in image coordinates, stable for months of use (\HwMonths), gone at a change of unit or gel (\HwOtherUnit); the hardware history of a dataset can be read from its images.
  \item We trace the collapse of classifiers trained with one object per session (\DOneSupRaw\pct\ on fifteen materials after a change of unit) to the hardware state acting as a class feature, with the classes recorded on the new unit as the sink.
  \item We show that a retrospective estimate from the contact stream removes the state as well as a blank does (\DOneSupPrev--\DOneSupBlank\pct\ on the cross-unit materials), where normalisation statistics fail; that re-rendering under it adapts frozen encoders without labels (\IjepaAdAcc\pct); and where it stops (five rigid materials across a gel replacement).
  \item We release a four-month VBTS dataset with two units, three gels, 20 materials, co-recorded force, bare-gel frames and a leave-one-campaign-out protocol.
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Vision-based tactile sensors (VBTS) such as GelSight~\cite{yuan2017gelsight,dong2017improved} and DIGIT~\cite{lambeta2020digit} render contact as an image: an elastomer gel is pressed against a surface, and internal LEDs shade its deformation for a camera. Because they are small, cheap and give a dense image of the contact, they have become the fingertips of robot hands: DIGIT fingertips on an Allegro hand reconstruct and track objects during in-hand manipulation~\cite{suresh2024neuralfeels}, multi-fingered hands rotate unseen objects from vision and touch~\cite{qi2023rotateit}, a multimodal VBTS fingertip is now a product-grade sensor~\cite{lambeta2024digit360}, a GelSight Mini on a hand-held gripper conditions manipulation policies on force~\cite{helmut2025tactilediffusion}, and tactile foundation models pretrained on millions of VBTS frames are available~\cite{higuera2024sparsh,t3fota2024,anytouch2025}. In these roles a VBTS is in contact for months: it is powered up and down, its gel is lubricated, worn and eventually replaced. Yet almost all learned VBTS perception is developed and evaluated inside a short recording campaign. Whether a tactile representation still works on the same sensor months later is, to our knowledge, untested: no public VBTS dataset spans months of one unit's use with a later campaign held out, and the closest proxies swap units or gels~\cite{sitr2025,tacverse2026,bideta2026} or span two weeks~\cite{tactilemnist2025}.

We recorded 2{,}711 grasps of 20 materials with one GelSight Mini over four months, in four campaigns separated by weeks, with the normal force co-recorded by a force sensor on the opposing finger, and, in the last campaign, bare-gel frames before and between grasps. Holding out the last campaign reveals a failure that no cross-unit study predicts. A supervised encoder trained in June--July recognises materials in September--October at \SupFoneRawSAcc\pct\ (chance 5\pct) and assigns \SupFoneRawSTop\pct\ of all episodes to a single class; frozen Sparsh encoders~\cite{higuera2024sparsh}, pretrained on GelSight Mini data, do no better (\SdinoRawAcc--\IjepaRawAcc\pct). Time is not the cause: held out \emph{within} the June--July campaign, sessions the same encoder never saw, recorded weeks apart, are recognised at \HoldSupRawAcc\pct.

The cause is measurable without any model (\cref{fig:signature}). At a fixed image position, the fine-scale content of a contact image correlates with the average of \emph{other objects touched in the same campaign} at \SigSameDay\ within a day and \SigSameEraOtherDay\ across its days (median over sessions), almost as much as with the same session's other contacts (\SigOwn), and only at \SigOtherEra\ with another campaign's. The pattern is present in the bare gel, stays fixed in image coordinates while the membrane moves under load, and is shared most by thick compliant samples that cannot transmit a backing: it is a rendering state of the imaging chain, not of the object or the fixture. Because a recording session holds one object, any material classifier can satisfy its labels with this state, and does; an era classifier applied to June contacts re-rendered under a July sensor state calls them July (\cref{sec:signature}).

This paper proposes to represent touch \emph{statefully}, and makes four contributions.
\textbf{(1) A stateful tactile representation.} Every observation is factored into the sensor's state and the touch (\cref{sec:signature}). Two states are enough for the three quantities we test, and both are estimated from the robot's own stream: the \emph{loaded} state, the position-wise mean of any recent contacts (the previous object, or the deployment so far), for appearance; the \emph{unloaded} state, the bare gel between grasps, for geometry and contact. The factoring applies on top of any encoder---a supervised network or a frozen tactile foundation model---and subtracting the loaded state recovers every encoder after the state change (frozen Sparsh \num{3}$\times$; a supervised CNN \SupFoneRawSAcc$\rightarrow$\SupFonePrevSAcc\pct, \SrcSupBlankAcc\pct\ with the bare gel as the estimate) while costing nothing when the state has not changed---which test-time normalisation does not achieve.
\textbf{(2) The state is physically real and measurable.} We show that a contact image carries its campaign's sensor state as a position-locked pattern, characterise how it persists within a campaign and changes across campaigns and at gel replacement (\cref{fig:state}), replicate it on a second unit in public data, and validate the factorisation directly: June contacts re-rendered under a July state are classified as July.
\textbf{(3) The factoring is learnable without labels.} Re-rendering training contacts under other sessions' states, with position-matched decoys as negatives, trains a small adapter that lifts frozen Sparsh-I-JEPA from \IjepaRawAcc\ to \IjepaAdAcc\pct\ with no labels and no data from the deployment campaign.
\textbf{(4) Consequences and a benchmark.} Appearance needs the loaded state removed; geometry needs the unloaded state: normal force from marker spreading against the bare gel transfers across months ($r$=\ForceGeomXeraC) where every appearance-based estimate fails ($R^2\le\ForceLearnedBestR$), and contact detection needs the same reference. We release a same-unit tactile benchmark with the test campaign months after training: four campaigns over four months, 20 materials, co-recorded force, bare-gel frames, power cycles and a gel replacement, with deployment-realistic protocols.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A vision-based tactile sensor (VBTS) presses a soft elastomer gel onto a surface and photographs the gel's deformation with a camera behind it~\cite{yuan2017gelsight,dong2017improved,lambeta2020digit}. One touch thus records the shape of the contact, the forces on it, and the texture of the surface at a resolution of tens of micrometres. Inexpensive units, such as the GelSight Mini and DIGIT, have made the sensor common on robot grippers. Robots use these images to predict whether a grasp will hold~\cite{calandra2017feeling}, to detect slip~\cite{dong2017improved}, to manipulate objects in the hand~\cite{she2021cable,suresh2024neuralfeels,qi2023rotateit} and to recognise what they touch~\cite{li2013texture,yuan2017connecting,yang2022touchandgo}. Because the output is an image, the models come from computer vision, and encoders pretrained on millions of tactile frames now serve all of these tasks~\cite{higuera2024sparsh,t3fota2024,unitouch2024,anytouch2025}.

This paper studies one of these tasks, recognizing the material of a surface from a single grasp. The material tells a robot how a surface will slip, how hard it may be squeezed, and where an object should be sorted. Material recognition is a standard benchmark for tactile encoders~\cite{higuera2024sparsh,unitouch2024,anytouch2025}, and is also the task most exposed to the problem we describe. The shape of a contact changes the image strongly, but what separates one material from another is faint, fine detail; the sensor adds faint, fine detail of its own. A robot keeps its sensors for months, during which the gel is lubricated and worn, and eventually the gel, a consumable part, is replaced, as is a unit that fails~\cite{davis2025resilience,cottrell2026durable}. If a classifier depends on the hardware, such routine maintenance breaks it, and the robot has no labels with which to notice.

The camera, the LEDs and the gel cartridge leave a faint mark of their own in every image, which we call the sensor's \emph{hardware state}. It has two parts, a fine pattern carried by the gel cartridge and a smooth, uneven illumination. We recorded \EpRecorded\ grasps of 20 materials over four months with two GelSight Mini units and three gels, and \cref{fig:signature} shows how strong the fine pattern is in them. At a fixed image position, the fine detail of a contact correlates at \HwFarWeeks\ with other objects pressed on the same unit and gel weeks later and at \HwFarMonths\ three months later, but only at \HwFarOtherUnit\ and \HwFarOtherGel\ with a different unit or gel. The images alone show when the hardware changed, and they corrected our laboratory log. A model trained to recognize materials therefore also sees cues identifying the sensor hardware; we investigate whether it relies on these cues and how that affects recognition when the hardware changes.

That the sensor appears in the image is known, and three practices deal with it. The first subtracts a frame of the unloaded gel, captured once or at intervals~\cite{gelslim2018,tactic2026,higuera2024sparsh,sitr2025,anytouch2026two,htt2026}. The second diversifies the training data across units and optics~\cite{suresh2024neuralfeels,tactic2026,sitr2025}. The third avoids raw appearance, for example by reading marker displacements, which survive ``damage or gel replacements''~\cite{rdp2025}. The evaluations behind these practices use one collection, or several units recorded at about the same time~\cite{sitr2025,bideta2026,feats2025,tacverse2026,rct2026,anytouch2026two}, and the longest span we found is three weeks~\cite{tactilemnist2025}. None follows one unit and gel for months, and none asks what a change of hardware does to a classifier. Medical imaging provides an example of how acquisition conditions can become shortcuts for classification. A network trained to detect pneumonia in chest X-rays learned hospital-specific cues associated with differences in pneumonia prevalence, reducing its accuracy on images from previously unseen hospitals~\cite{zech2018hospital,koh2021wilds}.

A classifier can exploit hardware cues when the training data associate particular materials with particular sensors. Many tactile datasets record each object in a separate session; in our training data, each material appears under only one hardware configuration--a sensor unit and its gel. Every image therefore contains both the material's texture and a hardware signature associated with a subset of the material classes. The classifier can use that signature to narrow its predictions without relying on texture alone.
In June and July, unit 2 recorded only four of the 20 materials. For a classifier trained on those recordings, its hardware signature therefore predicts one of those four classes. All September and October recordings were collected with unit 2, including materials previously recorded with unit 1. A ResNet-50 trained on June and July assigns \XpIntroUnitTwoShare\pct\ of these test episodes to the four classes associated with unit 2. Its accuracy on the sixteen materials learned from unit 1 is only \XpIntroUnitAcc\pct, compared with 5\pct\ chance accuracy. Frozen Sparsh encoders~\cite{higuera2024sparsh} show the same failure (supplement).
To test whether the hardware signature drives this bias, we introduce a controlled association between object class and sensor unit in SITR~\cite{sitr2025}, which records the same 20 objects with four GelSight Minis. We construct a training set in which one object appears only on the Mini used for testing, while the remaining objects appear only on another Mini. The classifier then assigns \CfSitrRawOneTop\pct\ of the test images to the single object associated with the test Mini, and accuracy on the remaining objects falls from \CfSitrRawZeroCross\ to \CfSitrRawOneCross\pct\ (\cref{sec:confound}).


The cure is to read touch together with the state of the sensor, and a robot can do this with its own stream of contacts. At test time, the robot averages the contacts it has made since the hardware last changed into a running estimate of the state, as microscopy estimates a flat-field from its own images~\cite{singh2014illumination,peng2017basic}, and subtracts the smooth part of that estimate from every image (\cref{alg:tracker}). A detector dates the changes of hardware from whole frames, so that the estimate restarts when the unit or the gel is replaced. This reading needs no labels and no training, so it can be added to any classifier. If the encoder can be retrained, the same stream serves as the target domain of a conditional adversary~\cite{long2018cdan}, so that the encoder's features stop separating the old hardware from the new. This alignment also reaches the fine pattern, which the reading leaves in place.

We evaluate on 55 sessions, each recorded on hardware other than the one its material was learned on. The reading alone raises a ResNet-50 from \XpRecipeAcc\ to \XpReadAcc\pct. That matches the best of five published test-time adaptation methods on the robot's stream, where four of them collapse because each batch holds a single material. Added to five published domain adaptation methods, the reading improves each of them, and with alignment the ResNet-50 reaches \XpFullAcc\pct. The gains hold for four encoders. The reading removes only an additive state, and it lowers the accuracy on the eight sessions that cross only a gel. SITR's Minis also differ in their field of view and contrast, and there the reading does not help a CNN trained from scratch, but alignment does.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item A four-month measurement of the hardware state of a VBTS: present in the bare gel, stable with use, replaced with the unit or the gel.
  \item Evidence that material classifiers trained with one object per session use this state as a shortcut, on our recordings and, under control, on a public dataset.
  \item A method that reads touch relative to the state using only the robot's unlabelled contacts, compared with eleven adaptation methods and four encoders, with its limits shown.
  \item A four-month VBTS dataset with two units, three gels, 20 materials, co-recorded force and bare-gel frames, which we release with a cross-hardware protocol and the code.
\end{itemize}

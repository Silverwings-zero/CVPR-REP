\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A vision-based tactile sensor (VBTS) presses a soft elastomer gel onto a surface and photographs the gel's deformation with a camera behind it~\cite{yuan2017gelsight,dong2017improved,lambeta2020digit}. One touch thus records the shape of the contact, the forces on it, and the texture of the surface at a resolution of tens of micrometers. Inexpensive units, such as the GelSight Mini and DIGIT, have made the sensor common on robot grippers. Robots use these images to predict whether a grasp will hold~\cite{calandra2017feeling}, detect slip~\cite{dong2017improved}, manipulate objects in the hand~\cite{she2021cable,suresh2024neuralfeels,qi2023rotateit} and recognize what they touch~\cite{li2013texture,yuan2017connecting,yang2022touchandgo}. Because the output is an image, the models come from computer vision, and encoders pretrained on millions of tactile frames now serve all of these tasks~\cite{higuera2024sparsh,t3fota2024,unitouch2024,anytouch2025}.

This paper studies one of these tasks, recognizing the material of a surface from a single grasp. The material tells a robot how a surface will slip, how hard it may be squeezed, and where an object should be sorted. Material recognition is a standard benchmark for tactile encoders~\cite{higuera2024sparsh,unitouch2024,anytouch2025}, and is also the task most exposed to the problem we describe. The shape of a contact changes the image strongly, but what separates one material from another is faint, fine detail; the sensor adds faint, fine detail of its own. A robot keeps its sensors for months, during which the gel is lubricated and worn, and eventually the gel, a consumable part, is replaced, as is a unit that fails~\cite{davis2025resilience,cottrell2026durable}. If a classifier depends on the hardware, such routine maintenance breaks it, and the robot has no labels with which to notice.

The camera, the LEDs and the gel cartridge leave a faint mark of their own in every image, which we call the sensor's \emph{hardware state}. It has two parts, a fine pattern carried by the gel cartridge and a smooth, uneven illumination. We recorded \EpRecorded\ grasps of 20 materials over four months with two GelSight Mini units and three gels, and \cref{fig:signature} shows how strong the fine pattern is in them. At a fixed image position, the fine detail of a contact correlates at \HwFarWeeks\ with other objects pressed on the same unit and gel weeks later and at \HwFarMonths\ three months later, but only at \HwFarOtherUnit\ and \HwFarOtherGel\ with a different unit or gel. A model trained to recognize materials therefore also sees cues identifying the sensor hardware; we investigate whether it relies on these cues and how that affects recognition when the hardware changes.

Tactile pipelines already compensate for the sensor's appearance (\cref{sec:related}), but no study follows one unit and gel for months or tests whether a classifier relies on hardware cues. Medical imaging provides an example of how acquisition conditions can become shortcuts for classification. A network trained to detect pneumonia in chest X-rays learned hospital-specific cues associated with differences in pneumonia prevalence, reducing its accuracy on images from previously unseen hospitals~\cite{zech2018hospital,koh2021wilds}.

A classifier can exploit hardware cues when the training data associate particular materials with particular sensors. Many tactile datasets record each object in a separate session; in our training data, each material appears under only one hardware configuration---a sensor unit and its gel. Every image therefore contains both the material's texture and a hardware signature associated with a subset of the material classes. The classifier can use that signature to narrow its predictions without relying on texture alone.
In June and July, unit 2 recorded only four of the 20 materials. All September and October recordings were collected with unit 2, including materials previously recorded with unit 1. A ResNet-50 trained on June and July assigns \XpIntroUnitTwoShare\pct\ of these test episodes to the four classes associated with unit 2. Its accuracy on the sixteen materials learned from unit 1 is only \XpIntroUnitAcc\pct, compared with 5\pct\ chance accuracy. Frozen Sparsh encoders~\cite{higuera2024sparsh} show the same failure (supplement).
To test whether the hardware signature drives this bias, we use SITR~\cite{sitr2025}, which records the same 20 objects with four GelSight Minis, and train with one object recorded only on the test Mini and the others only on another Mini. The classifier then assigns \CfSitrRawOneTop\pct\ of the test images to that one object, and accuracy on the remaining objects falls from \CfSitrRawZeroCross\ to \CfSitrRawOneCross\pct\ (\cref{sec:confound}).


The remedy is to account for the state of the sensor, and a robot can do this with its own stream of contacts. At test time, the robot averages its contacts since the hardware last changed into a running estimate of the state and subtracts its smooth part from every image (\cref{fig:overview}). A detector restarts the estimate when the unit or gel is replaced. This image correction needs no labels and no training, so it can be added to any classifier. If the encoder can be retrained, the same stream serves as the target domain of a conditional adversary~\cite{long2018cdan}, so that the encoder's features stop separating the old hardware from the new. This alignment also reaches the fine pattern, which the correction leaves in place.

We test on 55 sessions in which a material is touched with a different unit or gel than in training. On these sessions, the correction alone raises a ResNet-50 from \XpRecipeAcc\ to \XpReadAcc\pct, which matches the best of five published test-time adaptation methods, and it improves each of five published domain adaptation methods. With alignment it reaches \XpFullAcc\pct, and the gains hold for four encoders. The correction removes only an additive state. It lowers the accuracy on the eight sessions that cross only a gel, and on SITR's Minis, which also differ in field of view and contrast, a CNN trained from scratch needs alignment.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}
  \item A four-month measurement of the hardware state: in the bare gel, stable with use, replaced with the unit or gel.
  \item Evidence that material classifiers trained with one object per session use this state as a shortcut, on our recordings and, under control, on a public dataset.
  \item A method that corrects tactile images for the state using only the robot's unlabeled contacts, compared with eleven adaptation methods and four encoders, and its limits.
  \item A four-month dataset released with protocol and code: two units, three gels, 20 materials, force, bare-gel frames.
\end{itemize}

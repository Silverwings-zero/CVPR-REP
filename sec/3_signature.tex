\section{The hardware state in a tactile image}
\label{sec:signature}

We first characterize the image structure associated with the sensor hardware and examine how it changes with use and hardware replacement. We then test whether classifiers exploit this structure when material classes are associated with particular units or gels.

\paragraph{Data.}
A GelSight Mini (1640$\times$1232 native frames, \SI{11.3}{\micro\metre}/px, \SI{18.8}{fps}, printed markers) is mounted on one finger of a parallel gripper, and an ATI Nano17 force-torque sensor on the other finger measures the normal force. The object is a box whose face carries one of 20 materials: ABS plastic, aluminum foil, a bubble envelope, canvas, cardboard, ceramic, cork, EPDM foam, fiberglass, fleece, glitter paper, the two faces of a piece of leather, pleather, polyethylene foam, r\'esum\'e paper, silk, textured rubber, vinyl, and wood. Each episode grasps the face at one of two forces (40 or 80\pct\ of a per-material maximum, 0.9--5\,N) and strokes it at 2\,cm in the four cardinal directions and in a circle. The gel is lubricated every five episodes, and one session records one material. Four data collection campaigns cover four months: A (June 15--22), B (July 6--8), C (July 9--13) and D (September 22 to October 15), \EpRecorded\ episodes in all. \EpNoCrops\ episodes of campaign D have no eight-frame span of contact inside the stroke, so the material experiments use \EpCrops. Campaign D also records frames of the bare gel. The camera's exposure and white balance are fixed (on a held contact the mean of each colour channel drifts by at most 0.3 grey levels per episode), and we never resample a frame.

Two units and three gels were used. Unit 1 carried gel 1 until July 9, unit 2 carried gel 2 from July 10, and gel 3 replaced gel 2 on October 9 between 18:42 and 19:37. We call these three combinations of unit and gel the hardware configurations S1, S2 and S3.

\paragraph{Measurement.}
For every session we form the position-wise mean of its contact crops in frame coordinates, its \emph{mean contact frame} $M_s(p)$. For a sampled contact crop we correlate its fine band (190--370\,\si{\micro\metre}) with the same band of another session's mean frame, excluding pixels within 60\,px of a printed marker so that inpainting residue cannot contribute. \Cref{fig:signature} shows the medians over sessions. A contact matches the other contacts of its own session, which include the same object, at \HwFarOwn. It matches another object touched the same day at \HwFarSameDay, the same unit and gel on another day at \HwFarWeeks, and the same unit and gel three months later at \HwFarMonths. It does not match the other unit (\HwFarOtherUnit) or the other gel of the same unit (\HwFarOtherGel). Frames of the bare gel, with nothing touching it, match their session's mean contact frame at \BlankVsFieldFar. Another lab's GelSight Mini shows the same ordering (orange in \cref{fig:signature}).

\paragraph{What the state is.}
\Cref{fig:stateimages} shows the state's two components: a fine spatial pattern and a smooth illumination field. We use two tests to investigate the origin of the fine pattern. First, warping a bare-gel frame by the measured membrane displacement reduces its correlation with the session's mean contact frame from \BlankVsFieldFar\ to \WarpedBlankVsFieldFar. This suggests that the pattern remains fixed in the camera's view rather than moving with the membrane, although the small median displacement of \WarpDispPx\,px limits the strength of this evidence. Second, replacing the gel while retaining the same unit and camera removes the pattern, linking it to the gel cartridge. Together, these observations suggest a cartridge-associated pattern that remains approximately fixed in image coordinates, consistent with a non-uniform coating.
The smooth component varies modestly between sessions on the same hardware. Within a session, the illumination field changes by a median of \DriftUse\ grey levels over \DriftUseContacts\ grasps. Between the last bare-gel frame of one session and the first of the next, it changes by \DriftIdle\ grey levels despite no intervening contact. The larger between-session change shows that the field can vary while the sensor is idle, rather than only through use. Within a hardware configuration, each session's illumination field differs from the configuration mean by \LpDistWithinSthree--\LpDistWithinSone\ grey levels. Differences between configurations are larger: \LpDistSoneStwo\ grey levels between the two units and \LpDistStwoSthree\ between the two gels on unit 2.

\paragraph{The images record the hardware history.}
Changes in the hardware signature are abrupt enough to locate from the images alone. Pairwise correlations among the 35 June and July sessions reveal two distinct blocks, separated between July 9 and 10, with no other clear transition. The September and October sessions show a second transition on October 9. Our laboratory log records one unit change and four gel replacements, but only these two transitions are evident in the images. Operators corroborated both transitions using their notes; the remaining logged changes have no detectable image signature. We therefore use the image-derived timeline for our analysis. Because the laboratory log does not provide a reliable independent reference, we evaluate the online change detector described in \cref{sec:tracker} on spliced recording streams with change points known by construction (\cref{sec:trackerexp}).

\paragraph{The state as a shortcut.}
\label{sec:confound}
In our training data each material appears under one hardware configuration, because each was recorded in its own session, so a classifier can use the state as a feature of the materials recorded under it. The introduction tests this by tying classes to one of SITR's four Minis~\cite{sitr2025}, and \cref{fig:confound} shows the test for every number of tied classes. On Real Tactile MNIST~\cite{tactilemnist2025} we tie digits to gels instead, and tying one digit to the test gel sends \CfTmdRawOneTop\pct\ of the test touches to that digit.

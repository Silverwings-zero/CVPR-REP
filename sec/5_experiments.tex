\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
On our recordings, the main split trains on campaigns A, B and C (June and July, \EpTrainABC\ episodes) and tests on campaign D (September and October, \EpTestD\ episodes, 20 materials, chance 5\pct). The test set holds three hardware relations. Fifteen materials were learned on unit 1 and are tested on unit 2. Four rigid materials (cork, fibreglass, ABS, ceramic) were learned on unit 2 in July and are tested on its replaced gel. Wood was learned on unit 1 and is tested on the replaced gel. Two of the fifteen materials, textured rubber and EPDM foam, were replaced by new pieces of the same kind before campaign D. Every CNN input is a single native 192-px crop, twelve per episode, and the logits of an episode's crops are summed. We report macro accuracy, macro-F1, and the share of test episodes assigned to the most predicted class, which exposes a collapse onto few classes. The CNN is a four-stage convolutional encoder at native resolution, trained with cross-entropy for 12 epochs (supplement). Its jitter variant adds per-channel gains ($\pm$20\pct) and a smooth multiplicative field ($\pm$15\pct) during training. Heads are MLPs on frozen embeddings with three seeds, and CNN numbers average three training seeds. Whenever test images are read relative to a state estimate, training images are read relative to the mean of the other training sessions of their hardware epoch (\cref{sec:method}); otherwise both are read raw. Sparsh~\cite{higuera2024sparsh} receives its own two-frame input (two frames of the crop, five apart). All design choices were made on this split, and \cref{sec:folds} reports every other campaign as a test set.

\subsection{The shortcut under control}
\label{sec:confound}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{fig/fig_confound.pdf}
  \caption{\textbf{Tying classes to hardware, under control.} SITR~\cite{sitr2025} pressed 20 objects on four GelSight Minis. We train on $20-k$ classes recorded on one Mini and $k$ classes recorded only on the test Mini, and test all classes on the test Mini (frozen DINOv2, MLP head; mean over 12 Mini pairs and class draws, band: 10th--90th percentile over pairs).}
  \label{fig:confound}
\end{figure}

Our recordings tie each object to one hardware state, because each object was recorded in its own session. To test whether this tie causes the failure, we inject it into a dataset that does not have it. SITR~\cite{sitr2025} pressed the same 20 objects on four GelSight Minis. For a pair of Minis we train on $20-k$ classes recorded on one Mini and on $k$ classes recorded only on the other, and we test every class on the other Mini (\cref{fig:confound}). With no class tied, a frozen DINOv2 with an MLP head recognises the classes learned on the first Mini at \CfSitrRawZeroCross\pct. Tying a single class to the test Mini drops this to \CfSitrRawOneCross\pct, and \CfSitrRawOneTop\pct\ of all test images fall into that one class. With four tied classes the accuracy is \CfSitrRawFourCross\pct. Reading each image relative to its sensor's mean image raises the $k=1$ case to \CfSitrStreamOneCross\pct\ (paired bootstrap over pairs and draws, $+$\CfSitrStreamOneCiLo\ to $+$\CfSitrStreamOneCiHi\ points), and re-rendering every training image under the other Mini's state raises it to \CfSitrSaugOneCross\pct. On Real Tactile MNIST~\cite{tactilemnist2025}, where the tie is between digits and gels, the effect is weaker. Tying two digits to the test gel moves a CNN from \CfTmRawZeroCross\ to \CfTmRawTwoCross\pct\ on the other digits (supplement). The additive corrections also have a limit. A CNN trained from scratch on the confounded SITR set learns the Mini from cues that neither correction removes. It scores \CfSitrCnnRawCross\pct\ on the other classes, and \CfSitrCnnSaugCross\pct\ with state augmentation (supplement).

\subsection{Material after a change of hardware}
\label{sec:material}

\Cref{tab:material} shows the same failure on our recordings. Read raw, the CNN scores \SrcSupNoneAcc\pct\ and puts \SrcSupNoneTop\pct\ of the test episodes into a single class, one of the four materials it saw on unit 2. The fifteen materials it learned on unit 1 score \DOneSupRaw\pct. Frozen Sparsh encoders collapse in the same way. The jitter CNN collapses much less and scores \SrcAugNoneAcc\pct.

Both tools of \cref{sec:method} remove most of the collapse. With \cref{alg:tracker} the CNN reaches \FoDSupTrkStateAcc\pct\ (paired bootstrap over materials against raw, $+$\FoDSupCiLo\ to $+$\FoDSupCiHi\ points), and the jitter CNN \FoDAugTrkStateAcc\pct. This is as good as the two hand-picked references, the previous session's state and a bare-gel frame of the same session. State augmentation alone, with raw test images, raises the CNN to \FoDSaugSupRawAcc\pct. Combined with \cref{alg:tracker}, it gives \FoDSaugSupTrkStateAcc\pct\ for the CNN and \FoDSaugAugTrkStateAcc\pct\ with jitter. Frozen Sparsh-I-JEPA rises from \IjepaRawAcc\ to \IjepaTrkStAcc\pct\ without any training of the encoder.

The adaptation baselines do less. Re-estimating BatchNorm statistics per hardware state reaches \TtaSupAdabnEpochsAcc\pct\ even with the true change dates and all of the state's test images. TENT reaches \TtaSupTentIidAcc\pct\ when given mixed batches that a robot would not have, and it collapses (\TtaSupTentAcc\pct) on the robot's stream, where each batch holds one object. LAME, which smooths predictions over neighbouring test samples, gives \TtaSupLameAcc\pct. Label-shift correction lowers the accuracy to \TtaSupLabelshiftAcc\pct, because it finds the absorbing class frequent. These methods assume that a batch mixes classes or that the class priors carry the shift, and one object per session breaks both assumptions~\cite{burns2021bn,gong2022note}.

\Cref{tab:rotation} holds out each campaign in turn and reads every test campaign with \cref{alg:tracker}. Where the test hardware was seen in training for every test material (folds A and B), the reading neither helps nor costs much. An earlier version of our method read training images relative to a single previous session and lost up to ten points here. That session is one object, so its mean carries the object's imprint, and it sometimes lies on other hardware. Reading against the mean of the other sessions of the same hardware removes this loss. The gain in fold D is a gain against the shortcut. Trained on A and B alone, with all fifteen of their materials on unit 1, the CNN collapses less on D (\TsABSupRawAcc\pct\ raw over all 20 test materials, at most 75\pct\ possible). Trained on June alone, it scores \GapFoneSupBRawAcc\pct\ on its fifteen materials weeks later on the same unit and \GapFoneSupDRawAcc\pct\ on the other unit, a loss that no state estimate recovers.

\paragraph{The gel replacement.}
Fold C tests the four rigid materials on gel 2 after they were learned on gel 3, and wood on unit 1 after it was learned on unit 2. The reading helps the jitter CNN there (\FoCAugRawAcc\ to \FoCAugTrkStateAcc\pct) but not the plain CNN. To test whether the change of gel is additive, we re-rendered the gel-2 test crops under the state of gel 3, measured as the difference between the two gels' bare-gel frames with no contact involved. This raises the four materials from \GelRrCSupRaw\ to \GelRrCSupRr\pct\ for the plain CNN, so the gel change is largely additive. The tracker fails there for a simpler reason. Fold C's test sessions are the first sessions on the new hardware, so the tracker has almost no other objects to average. In fold D the same four materials score well raw only because the collapse sends many episodes to them. Raw, cork and fibreglass are recognised at \GelRrDSupRecCork\ and \GelRrDSupRecFiber\pct, but only \GelRrDSupPrecCork\ and \GelRrDSupPrecFiber\pct\ of the episodes assigned to them are correct.

Run over all \EpRecorded\ episodes in recording order (\cref{fig:tracker}), the detector raises \FrAlarms\ alarms. One comes \FrUnitLatency\ episodes after the change of unit and one \FrGelLatency\ episodes after the gel replacement. The other \FrFalseAlarms\ are false: one in the second September session, after the summer break, and one the day before the gel replacement. \Cref{tab:detect} applies the same rule to streams spliced from four datasets, with thresholds set on one unit of each and nothing tuned on change points. It detects \TeOursFramesDet\pct\ of the changes spliced from our recordings: \TeOursFramesUnitDet\pct\ of those that involve the other unit and \TeOursFramesGelDet\pct\ of the gel changes on the same unit. On the public datasets it detects \TePubDetMin--\TePubDetMax\pct, with \TePubFaMax\ or fewer false alarms per 1{,}000 items everywhere. Our first implementation computed the statistics on the sparse material crops. It missed the change of unit on the real stream and detected only \TeOursDet\pct\ of the spliced changes. We moved to whole frames after these failures and kept the rule, so the real-stream result above is not an independent test, while the spliced public streams are.

The detector matters for the change of unit. A running mean that is never restarted reads the September sessions against a mixture of both units, and the CNN reaches \SrcSupNoResetAcc\pct. Started at the deployment in September, after the change of unit, the reading gives \FoDSupSepRestStateAcc\pct\ with restarts and \FoDSupSepNoRestStateAcc\pct\ without, so the running mean could also have absorbed the gel replacement. Weighting by object rather than by contact helps the CNN (\FoDSupTrkStateAcc\ against \FoDSupUniStateAcc\pct), and restarting from the plain training mean instead of the old smooth illumination changes little (\FoDSupPriorGStateAcc\pct).

\paragraph{What the estimate needs.}\label{sec:sources}
Any unlabelled frames of the deployment work, provided they come from several objects (supplement). The session's own contacts are a worse estimate than other objects' contacts, because their mean contains the object. In a run with three seeds and five draws per size, an estimate built from one other object gives \CvsSupObjOne\pct, from two objects \CvsSupObjTwo\pct\ and from nineteen \CvsSupObjNineteen\pct. For the CNN the smooth illumination field alone does as well as the full field, and for the jitter CNN the fine pattern adds about four points.

\subsection{Other sensors}
\label{sec:second}
\paragraph{Foundation features encode the unit.}
Fourteen materials were recorded on both units. A linear probe trained on frozen features of some of these materials, and tested on the others, tells the two units apart from a single crop at \ProbeSdinoRawUnit\pct\ for Sparsh-DINO, \ProbeIjepaRawUnit\ for Sparsh-I-JEPA, \ProbeDinovRawUnit\ for DINOv2 and \ProbeCnnRawUnit\ for our CNN, where chance is 50\pct. Read relative to the previous session's state, the same crops give \ProbeSdinoPrevUnit, \ProbeIjepaPrevUnit, \ProbeDinovPrevUnit\ and \ProbeCnnPrevUnit\pct. The two units were also used in different seasons, which this probe cannot separate from the hardware.

\paragraph{Across GelSight Minis without a confound.}
On SITR's standard protocol, where every class appears on every Mini, a head trained on one Mini scores \SitrDinovRawIntra\pct\ on the other three raw, \SitrDinovBgIntra\pct\ with SITR's background image subtracted, and \SitrDinovStreamIntra\pct\ relative to the mean of 16{,}000 unlabelled images of each sensor. The mean of 100 random images gives \SitrDinovStreamHundredIntra\pct, and the mean of 20 is worse than no correction (\SitrDinovStreamTwentyIntra\pct). SITR itself needs 18 calibration presses per sensor and reports 90\pct\ on its 16-object version. On FEATS, no image-space reading brings normal force across units or gels close to the training error (supplement).

\paragraph{Normal force.}
Force is a matter of mechanics, and the state does not describe it. Whole-frame models trained on June and July to regress normal force from appearance reach $R^2\le\ForceLearnedBestR$ on September and October, even with the state removed. Marker displacements against a bare-gel grid recorded in September, fitted on June and July, predict the September and October sessions before the gel replacement at $r=\ForceGeomXeraC$ ($R^2=\ForceGeomXeraR$, supplement).

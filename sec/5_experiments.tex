\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

We evaluate whether accounting for hardware state improves material recognition when a sensor unit or gel is replaced. The experiments compare correction and feature alignment with existing adaptation methods, examine their contributions across encoders and datasets, and test automatic detection of hardware changes.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We evaluate material recognition on recordings collected with a different hardware configuration from the one used to learn that material. We use three folds, holding out campaign D, campaign C, and campaigns A and B together; each fold trains on the remaining campaigns (\cref{tab:folds}). Across these folds, 55 sessions qualify for evaluation, each tested once. In 47 sessions, the material was learned on a different unit and gel; in the remaining 8, it was learned on the same unit with a different gel. Each session contains one material.
All method choices, including the low-pass width and change-detector parameters, were selected using the fold that holds out D. The fold holding out A and B trains on later recordings and tests on earlier ones. Before training any model for this fold, we recorded a dated pre-registration specifying three directional predictions; all three were supported (supplement).
We compute accuracy within each session as the proportion of correctly classified episodes, then average equally over the 55 sessions. We report 95\pct\ confidence intervals obtained by bootstrapping sessions and paired bootstrap intervals for differences between methods. Chance accuracy is 5\pct. To reveal predictions concentrated on a small number of classes, we also report the proportion of test episodes assigned to the most frequently predicted class in each fold, averaged across folds.

\paragraph{Training and the stream.}
Unless stated otherwise, all methods use an ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-50~\cite{he2016resnet}, fine-tuned for 6{,}000 AdamW steps with batch size 64. Inputs are 192-px crops sampled along the contact at the native resolution of \SI{11.3}{\micro\metre}/px, which we call \emph{tube crops}. Augmentations include flips, quarter turns, and photometric jitter (supplement). We refer to this baseline training procedure as the \emph{recipe}. For each episode, we average the class log-probabilities across 24 crops: three contact positions in each of eight stroke frames.
Table entries average three training seeds; comparisons between the closest-performing methods use five. Across seeds, the 55-session mean accuracy varies by less than one percentage point for the recipe and image correction, and by one to three points for the aligned models. This variation is smaller than the confidence intervals obtained by bootstrapping sessions.
All adaptation methods receive the same unlabelled stream, consisting of all contacts recorded on the test hardware during the test campaign. The stream includes the test sessions themselves, as it would during robot operation. To assess performance on sessions excluded from adaptation, row~i of \cref{tab:ablation} uses a separate held-out portion of the stream. In the main comparisons, all methods receive the true hardware-change times, allowing image correction and test-time baselines to reset at the same points. \Cref{sec:trackerexp} evaluates replacing these times with the detector's alarms.

\subsection{Comparison with published adaptation methods}
\label{sec:baselines}

\Cref{tab:baselines} groups the methods by what they use. Every published method runs with its paper's default settings on the same encoder, training budget and stream. SDAT and MIC keep the better of two trade-off weights, judged on the first seed of this protocol, which favors them over our method, whose weight is fixed (supplement). Trained with the recipe, the ResNet-50 recognises the materials of the 55 sessions at \XpRecipeAcc\pct, and on average over the folds \XpRecipeTop\pct\ of the test episodes go to a single class.

\paragraph{Test time.}
Four of the five test-time adaptation methods achieve only about \XpTtaBnAcc\pct\ accuracy on the robot's stream. These methods—per-batch BatchNorm, Tent, SAR, and DeYO—estimate normalisation statistics from each batch of 72 crops drawn from three consecutive episodes. Because consecutive episodes belong to the same session, each batch contains only one material. Normalising within these batches can therefore suppress material-specific information along with hardware differences.
When episodes are shuffled within each hardware configuration, batch statistics better approximate configuration-wide statistics, as used by AdaBN~\cite{li2018adabn}. Under this shuffled setting, accuracy recovers to \XpTtaBnIidAcc\pct, slightly above the unadapted model. RoTTA, which was designed for correlated streams, is the only evaluated test-time adaptation method that improves accuracy on the original stream, gaining \XdRottaVsSrc\ percentage points over the unadapted model.
Our image correction achieves a comparable improvement without batches or gradient updates, raising accuracy from \XpSrcAcc\ to \XpSrcReadAcc\pct. Its advantage over RoTTA is \XdSrcReadVsRotta\ percentage points, but the confidence interval includes zero (\XdSrcReadVsRottaLo\ to \XdSrcReadVsRottaHi).
The aggregate results conceal different behavior across hardware changes. RoTTA improves recognition after a change of unit but reduces accuracy on the eight sessions involving only a gel change from \XpSrcGel\ to \XpTtaRottaGel\pct. Image correction produces a smaller decrease on those sessions, to \XpSrcReadGel\pct.

\paragraph{Retraining.}
All retraining methods outperform the baseline recipe. Adding image correction improves each method's mean accuracy across the 55 sessions by three to ten percentage points, with confidence intervals excluding zero (last column). These gains come from the 47 sessions involving a change of both unit and gel. On the eight sessions involving only a gel change, correction reduces accuracy for every method; we discuss this limitation in \cref{sec:discussion}.
With correction, DANN, SDAT, MIC, and CDAN+E perform within three percentage points of one another, while MCC remains substantially lower. Our combination of CDAN+E and correction achieves the highest mean accuracy, \XpFullAcc\pct. However, its advantage over corrected DANN is only \XdFullVsDannRead\ percentage points across five seeds, with a confidence interval that includes zero (\XdFullVsDannReadLo\ to \XdFullVsDannReadHi). Its advantages over corrected SDAT and MIC are \XdFullVsSdatRead\ and \XdFullVsMicRead\ points, respectively. Among these four aligners, adding image correction has a larger effect than the choice of alignment method.

\paragraph{Changing the training images instead.}
The published method closest to the reading is FDA~\cite{fda2020}, which gives every training image the low-frequency amplitude spectrum of an image from the stream, so that the model trains under the new hardware's illumination. With $\beta=0.01$, which swaps the band that the reading removes, FDA gains about six points over the recipe, half of what the reading gains without any retraining (\XdReadVsFda\ points between them, \XdReadVsFdaLo\ to \XdReadVsFdaHi). The reading adds about three points to FDA, and the wider band of $\beta=0.05$ gains less.

\subsection{What each part contributes}
\label{sec:ablation}

\Cref{tab:ablation} evaluates the contribution of each component. Image correction alone improves accuracy by about twelve percentage points without additional training (b). Feature alignment alone produces a larger gain (c), and combining the two outperforms either component alone (d). Replacing CDAN+E with DANN reduces accuracy by less than one point (e), indicating that the benefit is not specific to CDAN+E. Both components help on the two folds involving changes of sensor unit. Neither improves the fold holding out campaign C, which contains four sessions involving a gel change and one session of wood. Restricting correction to low spatial frequencies is essential. Subtracting the full estimated state, including its fine-scale pattern, reduces the full method's accuracy by about forty percentage points, well below the baseline recipe. Estimates formed from a few objects retain their contact imprints in the fine band, so subtracting this component can remove material information. Using $\sigma=8$ or $16$\,px, which allows more fine-scale structure into the correction, reduces accuracy by ten to twenty points. Larger values of $66$ or $132$\,px reduce accuracy by up to two points. The selected width was tuned on the fold holding out D; its 20 sessions are therefore included in this parameter sweep.

We also evaluate two additional objectives that encourage confident predictions while maintaining diversity across predicted classes. The first operates on the average prediction over several touches, analogous to MEMO's use of augmented views~\cite{zhang2022memo}. This grouped-confidence objective adds a few percentage points, but its confidence interval includes zero (f). The second encourages confidence for each touch individually, following SHOT~\cite{liang2020shot}, and reduces accuracy by about five points (g). We include neither objective in the final method. Correcting the training images as well as the test images, using estimates from other sessions on the same hardware, also provides no benefit and reduces accuracy by about two points (h).

The main alignment result is transductive: the unlabelled adaptation stream includes the sessions being evaluated (d). To test whether the benefit extends to later, unseen sessions, we align using only the first half of each hardware configuration's test sessions and evaluate on the second half (i). On these \XpLateN\ sessions, image correction alone achieves \XpLateReadAcc\pct. Alignment using only the earlier sessions raises accuracy to \XpLateEarlyAcc\pct, compared with \XpLateFullAcc\pct\ when the alignment stream also includes the evaluated sessions. Thus, approximately three quarters of the alignment gain remains when the evaluated sessions are excluded, supporting adaptation to shared hardware characteristics rather than dependence on the specific test contacts.

\subsection{Other encoders and views}
\label{sec:encoders}

\Cref{tab:encoders} evaluates different encoders, including a small CNN trained from scratch, under two crop settings. With tube crops, all encoders perform poorly under the baseline recipe. Image correction improves each by ten to twenty percentage points, and adding feature alignment further improves every encoder.
The frame view uses larger crops sampled anywhere in the image, exposing the encoder to more of the illumination field than tube crops. In this setting, alignment alone provides substantial gains, while the additional benefit of image correction depends on the encoder. Correction improves aligned DINOv2-B by \XdDinoFrameReadVsCdan\ percentage points (confidence interval: \XdDinoFrameReadVsCdanLo\ to \XdDinoFrameReadVsCdanHi). For ConvNeXt-T, it changes accuracy by about one point, with an interval including zero. For ResNet-50, it reduces accuracy by about nine points (\XdResFrameReadVsCdanLo\ to \XdResFrameReadVsCdanHi). The best frame-view model, ConvNeXt-T with alignment and correction, reaches \XpCnxFrameCdanReadAcc\pct, close to ResNet-50 with tube crops, although correction provides no clear additional benefit for that model.
Tube crops have been used throughout the experiments conducted since pre-registration; the frame-view comparison was added later as a robustness check. We report ResNet-50 in the preceding tables because it achieves the highest accuracy among the tube-crop encoders. The other three show the same ordering of the baseline, correction-only, and correction-plus-alignment conditions.

\subsection{Transfer across sensors with broader appearance differences}
\label{sec:second}

SITR's GelSight Minis differ in field of view and contrast, as well as additive image structure. Their cameras observe different regions of the gel, and their contrast differs by a factor of \SmContrastRatio\ (supplement). Image correction therefore leaves substantial sensor-specific information: after correction, a CNN distinguishes two SITR Minis with \UpSitrRead\pct\ accuracy on unseen objects. The same test on our two units achieves \UpOursRead\pct, compared with 50\pct\ chance accuracy.
We first evaluate image correction in the controlled setting where one class is associated exclusively with the test Mini. Subtracting that Mini's mean contact image, including both smooth and fine-scale components, raises accuracy on classes learned from the other Mini from \CfSitrRawOneCross\ to \CfSitrStreamOneCross\pct\ for a frozen DINOv2 encoder with an MLP head (\cref{fig:confound}). However, a CNN trained from scratch achieves only about \SmStreamCross\pct\ after the same correction (supplement). This is consistent with reliance on field-of-view and contrast differences that subtracting a shared image cannot remove.
Feature alignment addresses these differences at the representation level. In a separate experiment, we associate four classes exclusively with the test Mini and train a CNN from scratch, using unlabelled images from that Mini as the adaptation stream. CDAN+E raises accuracy on the 16 classes learned from the other Mini from \XsJit\ to \XsCdan\pct. Restricting the stream to 5 of the 20 classes reduces this gain by approximately half, showing that adaptation depends on the class coverage of the available contacts. A per-touch confidence objective also helps when the stream contains all 20 objects in balanced proportions, although it did not improve alignment on our dataset. Most of this additional benefit disappears when the stream contains only 5 classes.

\subsection{Dating the changes}
\label{sec:trackerexp}

Run over all \EpRecorded\ episodes in recording order, the detector raises \FrAlarms\ alarms: \FrUnitLatency\ episodes after the change of unit, \FrGelLatency\ episodes after the gel replacement, and \FrFalseAlarms\ false ones, in the second September session after the summer break and on the day before the gel replacement. We also splice recordings of one unit or gel onto another in four datasets (ours, SITR, FEATS and Tactile MNIST), with thresholds set on one unit of each and nothing tuned on change points. It detects \TeOursFramesDet\pct\ of the changes on the streams spliced from our recordings, \TeSitrMiniDet\pct\ between SITR's Minis, \TeFeatsDet\pct\ on FEATS and \TeTmnistDet\pct\ on Tactile MNIST, with at most \TeTmnistFa\ false alarms per thousand stream items (supplement). Restarting the estimate at the detector's alarms instead of the true dates does not lower the accuracy of the full method, which changes by \XdFullVarDetVsLp\ points (\XdFullVarDetVsLpLo\ to \XdFullVarDetVsLpHi).

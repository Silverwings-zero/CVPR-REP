\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\paragraph{Protocol.}
We test every recording of a material made on hardware other than the one it was learned on. Three folds hold out campaign D (September and October), campaign C (July 9--13), and campaigns A and B together (June and early July), and each trains on the remaining campaigns. Their test sessions on other hardware number 55. In 47 of them the material was learned on the other unit, and in 8 it was learned on the other gel of the same unit. The fold that holds out A and B is the reversed split whose predictions we wrote down before training any model for it (supplement). Each session holds one material. We report the share of a session's episodes that are classified correctly, averaged over the 55 sessions, with 95\pct\ intervals from a bootstrap over sessions, and paired bootstrap intervals for differences between methods. Chance is 5\pct. We also report the share of test episodes assigned to the most predicted class, which exposes a collapse onto few classes. Unless stated otherwise, every method uses the same encoder, a ResNet-50~\cite{he2016resnet} that starts from its ImageNet weights and is fine-tuned at the native \SI{11.3}{\micro\metre}/px on 192-px crops along the contact, with flips, quarter turns and photometric jitter. We call this training the recipe. An episode is classified from 24 crops. Table rows average three training seeds, and the comparisons between the closest methods use five. Every method that adapts receives the same unlabelled stream: all contacts recorded on the test hardware in the test campaign.

\subsection{The shortcut under control}
\label{sec:confound}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering

  \caption{\textbf{Tying classes to hardware, under control.} SITR~\cite{sitr2025} pressed 20 objects on four GelSight Minis. We train on $20-k$ classes recorded on one Mini and $k$ classes recorded only on the test Mini, and test all classes on the test Mini (frozen DINOv2, MLP head; mean over 12 Mini pairs and class draws, band: 10th--90th percentile over pairs).}
  \label{fig:confound}
\end{figure}

Our recordings tie each object to one hardware state, because each object was recorded in its own session. To test whether this tie causes the failure, we inject it into a dataset that does not have it. SITR~\cite{sitr2025} pressed the same 20 objects on four GelSight Minis. For a pair of Minis we train on $20-k$ classes recorded on one Mini and on $k$ classes recorded only on the other, and we test every class on the other Mini (\cref{fig:confound}). With no class tied, a frozen DINOv2 with an MLP head recognises the classes learned on the first Mini at \CfSitrRawZeroCross\pct. Tying a single class to the test Mini drops this to \CfSitrRawOneCross\pct\ on average over Mini pairs, and \CfSitrRawOneTop\pct\ of all test images fall into that one class. On Real Tactile MNIST~\cite{tactilemnist2025} we tie digits to gels instead. Tying one digit to the test gel sends \CfTmdRawOneTop\pct\ of the test touches to that digit. Our own recordings show the same failure. Trained with the recipe, the ResNet-50 recognises the materials of the 55 sessions at \XpRecipeAcc\pct, and in every fold about a quarter of the test episodes go to a single class. % TODO(r50): check the per-fold top-class share of r50_tube_geo_src before merging

\subsection{Comparison with published adaptation methods}
\label{sec:baselines}

\Cref{tab:baselines} groups the methods by what they use. Test-time methods adapt the trained model while the stream arrives. Retraining methods train again with the stream as unlabelled target data. Every published method runs with its paper's default settings on the same encoder, training budget and stream, and the retraining methods keep the better of two trade-off weights on seed 0 (supplement).

\paragraph{Test time.}
Four of the five test-time methods fall to chance on the robot's stream. Test-time BatchNorm, Tent, SAR and DeYO estimate normalisation statistics from the current batch, and consecutive episodes come from one session, so every batch holds a single material. The statistics then normalise the material away. RoTTA was designed for such correlated streams. It survives, but it does not improve on the unadapted model by a margin we can distinguish from zero. In their own benchmark setting, with the episodes of each hardware state shuffled, the BatchNorm family recovers to \XpTtaBnIidAcc\pct, still below the unadapted model, and RoTTA reaches \XpTtaRottaIidAcc\pct. The reading needs neither batches nor gradient steps. It raises the unadapted model from \XpSrcAcc\ to \XpSrcReadAcc\pct, which is \XdSrcReadVsRotta\ points above RoTTA on the robot's stream (interval \XdSrcReadVsRottaLo\ to \XdSrcReadVsRottaHi) and \XdSrcReadVsRottaIid\ points above RoTTA on the shuffled stream, a difference whose interval includes zero.

\paragraph{Retraining.}
Every retraining method improves on the recipe, and our reading improves almost every one of them further. Added to DANN, MCC and SDAT at test time, it gains between four and five points each, with intervals that exclude zero. MIC, which adds a masked-image consistency term to SDAT, does worse than SDAT on our data and gains little from the reading. CDAN+E is the strongest aligner here, and our method is CDAN+E with the reading. It reaches \XpFullAcc\pct. Over five seeds it is \XdFullVsDannRead\ points above DANN with the reading (\XdFullVsDannReadLo\ to \XdFullVsDannReadHi), and over three seeds \XdFullVsSdatRead\ points above SDAT with the reading (\XdFullVsSdatReadLo\ to \XdFullVsSdatReadHi) and \XdFullVsMccRead\ above MCC with the reading (\XdFullVsMccReadLo\ to \XdFullVsMccReadHi). The reading is not tied to our training. It helps whichever aligner sits under it.

\paragraph{Changing the training images instead.}
The published method closest to the reading is FDA~\cite{fda2020}. It gives every training image the low-frequency amplitude spectrum of an image from the stream, so that the model trains on images that carry the new hardware's illumination, and it needs neither an adversary nor a change at test time. We run it with $\beta=0.01$, which swaps the band that the reading removes, and with $\beta=0.05$, a wider band that its paper also uses (supplement). % TODO(r50): FDA numbers (wave 14, fda01 and fda05) and the sentence that reads them.

\subsection{What each part contributes}
\label{sec:ablation}

\Cref{tab:ablation} adds the parts one at a time. The reading alone gains about twelve points without any training (b). % TODO(r50): re-check every number word in this paragraph (low-pass variants from queue 27, DANN from queue 26, row h from wave 14). Its low-pass matters. Subtracting the whole estimate, fine pattern included, drops the accuracy to \XpReadVarFullAcc\pct, below the recipe, because the fine band of an estimate built from a few objects still carries their imprints. Narrower low-passes lose most of the gain, and wider ones lose a few points. Alignment alone gains more (c), and the two add up: the full method is better than either part alone (d). The gains do not depend on CDAN+E. DANN in its place loses a point or two (e). We also tried two confidence objectives on the stream, which ask the classifier to be confident on the new hardware while spreading its predictions over the classes. One averages the predictions of several touches before asking for confidence, as MEMO~\cite{zhang2022memo} averages over augmented copies of an image, and the other asks it of every touch, as SHOT~\cite{liang2020shot} does. Neither adds to alignment by a margin we can distinguish from zero (f, g), so the method does not include them. The reading belongs at test time. Reading the training images as well, each relative to a random subset of the other sessions of its hardware so that the model trains on the kind of image it is tested on, does not add to the method and costs about two points (h).

A robot that adapts to its own stream classifies some touches that the stream already contained. To separate adaptation from memorisation, we align with the first half of each hardware state's test sessions only and score the second half, which the stream never saw (i). The gain over the reading alone remains, at about two thirds of the gain that the whole stream gives on the same sessions.

\subsection{Other encoders and views}
\label{sec:encoders}

\Cref{tab:encoders} repeats the comparison with other encoders, among them a small CNN that trains from scratch at the native resolution on one small GPU. On the tube view, every encoder collapses under the recipe in the same way, and the reading lifts each of them by ten to twenty points. Alignment adds to the reading for every encoder. The frame view gives the encoders a larger field of view, crops of 384\,px placed anywhere in the frame. There, alignment carries most of the gain, and what the reading adds on top of it depends on the encoder. It adds \XdDinoFrameReadVsCdan\ points for DINOv2-B (\XdDinoFrameReadVsCdanLo\ to \XdDinoFrameReadVsCdanHi), changes ConvNeXt-T by a point within an interval that includes zero, and costs ResNet-50 nine points (\XdResFrameReadVsCdanLo\ to \XdResFrameReadVsCdanHi). A crop of the whole frame contains most of the illumination field, which the tube crops only sample, and the encoders differ in how they use it after alignment. The reading is meant for crops along the contact, where it helps every encoder, and that is where we use it. The best whole-frame model, ConvNeXt-T aligned and read, reaches \XpCnxFrameCdanReadAcc\pct, close to the ResNet-50 on tube crops, but there the second part of the method is idle. On tube crops both parts work for every encoder, which is why the paper's comparisons use that view.

SITR's Minis differ in more than an additive image. They see different parts of their gels and differ in contrast by a factor of \SmContrastRatio\ (supplement). A CNN trained to tell two Minis apart still does so for \UpSitrRead\pct\ of the images of unseen objects after the reading, while for our two units the same test falls to \UpOursRead\pct, where chance is 50\pct. The reading cannot repair a tie between classes and Minis, and a CNN trained from scratch on SITR with four classes tied to the test Mini collapses with or without it. Alignment works on the encoder rather than on the image, so it should still help, and it does (\cref{tab:sitr}). With the test Mini's unlabelled images as the stream, CDAN+E raises the accuracy on the classes learned on the other Mini from \XsJit\ to \XsCdan\pct. When the stream holds only 5 of the 20 classes, the gain shrinks by about half, so the stream must cover the materials that the robot is expected to recognise. A confidence objective on the stream, which did not add to alignment on our data (\cref{tab:ablation}), does add to it on SITR, with either aligner. SITR's stream is a balanced sample of all 20 objects, which is what the objective assumes when it spreads the predictions over the classes. With a stream of 5 classes, its gain over DANN alone falls to a few points. A robot's stream is of the second kind, because it holds only the materials the robot happened to touch after the change.

\subsection{Dating the changes}
\label{sec:trackerexp}

Run over all \EpRecorded\ episodes in recording order, the detector raises \FrAlarms\ alarms: \FrUnitLatency\ episodes after the change of unit, \FrGelLatency\ episodes after the gel replacement, and \FrFalseAlarms\ false ones, in the second September session after the summer break and on the day before the gel replacement. On streams spliced from four datasets, with thresholds set on one unit of each and nothing tuned on change points, it detects \TeOursFramesDet\pct\ of the changes from our recordings (supplement). A false alarm costs little, because the estimate restarts from the training mean and fills again with the next objects. Reading the test images with the estimate restarted at the detector's alarms instead of the true dates gives \XpReadVarDetAcc\pct\ for the recipe model, against \XpReadVarLpAcc\pct\ with the true dates.
